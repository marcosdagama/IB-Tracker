DP1 Term One Tracker

Six fortnightly sprints. Each one lists what should be acquired (can-do statements), a practice target, and a 1–4 self-check. Physics HL (Higher Level) and Maths AA SL (Analysis and Approaches, Standard Level) lead the plan (technology route); the other subjects and the Core (Theory of Knowledge, Creativity-Activity-Service and the Extended Essay) are kept on a steady, lighter track.

The fortnight cycle

Based on Zimmerman's self-regulated learning model (plan → perform → reflect).

  • P

    Plan, Monday wk 1 (15 min). Read the sprint's can-do list, block study slots in the calendar, flag anything already shaky.

  • D

    Do, both weeks. Retrieval first: close the notes and attempt questions, then check. Mix in 2–3 questions from earlier sprints each session.

  • R

    Reflect, Friday wk 2 (20 min, with a parent). Tick what is secure, rate each lead subject 1–4, write three lines, carry any 1s and 2s into the next sprint.

Self-check scale

1Can't do it yet, even with notes 2Can do it with notes or a worked example 3Can do exam-style questions without notes 4Can explain it to someone else and spot errors

Aim for 3+ on every lead-subject item by the end of the sprint after it was taught.

How to measure confidence

Feeling familiar with a topic after re-reading is not the same as being able to do it. Test, then rate.

  1. Pick 3 questions on the item that you haven't done before (question bank, textbook, or from the teacher).
  2. Close the notes and set a timer at roughly exam pace (about 1–1.5 minutes per mark).
  3. Score it: 0–1 right = 1. Right only after checking notes or an example = 2. 2–3 right with no notes = 3. All right, and you can explain the method aloud in under a minute or spot the mistake in a wrong worked answer = 4.
  4. Rate the subject for the sprint by its weakest important item, not the average, and write any 1s in the reflection.
  5. Retest 1–2 weeks later. A rating that survives the gap is real.

Confidence by sprint

Blank until rated. Two 2s in a row on a lead subject (Physics or Maths) = ask the teacher or tutor that week.

Study methods that work

From Dunlosky et al. (2013), a review of 10 study techniques.

Practice testing (high utility)

Close the book and write down everything you remember, then check. Do questions before re-reading the chapter, and try each worked example yourself before reading the solution. Flashcards count.

Distributed practice (high utility)

Three 40-minute sessions across a week beat one 2-hour session. Revisit a topic after about a day, a week, and three weeks. Anki does this scheduling automatically.

Interleaving (moderate)

Mix 3–4 topics in one problem set and hide the topic labels, so you have to choose the method yourself. It feels harder and works better, especially in maths and physics.

Self-explanation (moderate)

After each step of a solution, ask "why does this step work?" and answer in one sentence. Explaining to someone else is the strongest version.

Re-reading and highlighting (low utility)

They feel productive but do little. Turn highlights into questions on flashcards, and after reading a page, close it and recall the main points.

Every study session

Before, while and after you study

Before
  • Clear desk: only the book, notes, calculator and data booklet for this session.
  • Phone in another room. Even a silent phone on the desk pulls attention.
  • Quiet space: silence or steady background noise; no lyrics, no TV.
  • One clear goal written down, e.g. "10 suvat questions, 80% right".
  • Water within reach, good light, chair and screen at a comfortable height.
While
  • Blocks of 25–45 minutes with a 5–10 minute break away from screens.
  • Questions first, notes second. Try before you look.
  • Stuck for 10 minutes? Mark it, move on, and bring it to the teacher or tutor.
  • Hardest subject first, while your energy is highest.
  • Stand, stretch or walk during breaks.
After
  • Check answers against the markscheme and update the error log.
  • Two-minute recall: write the three main ideas of the session from memory.
  • Plan the next session so starting tomorrow is easy.
  • Tick the tracker; seeing progress keeps the habit going.
  • Screens off 30–60 minutes before bed.
Asking for help
  • Ask the teacher within 48 hours of getting stuck, before the next topic builds on it.
  • Bring specific questions ("step 3 of question 4"), not "I don't get it".
  • Use tutor time for items rated 1 or 2, with the error log open.
  • Study with a classmate and take turns explaining solutions.
Health
  • Sleep 8–10 hours, the range sleep specialists recommend for teenagers. Memory consolidates during sleep.
  • Move every day: sport, a walk, cycling. It also counts towards CAS.
  • Regular meals, and water through the day.
  • If stress stays high for more than a couple of weeks, talk to a parent, the tutor or the school counsellor.
Motivation
  • Start small: "just 10 minutes" is often enough to keep going.
  • Compare yourself with your past self: last sprint's ratings, not classmates.
  • Connect the work to the goal: the degree, the career, the university.
  • Plan a reward after a finished block, not before.
  • Aim for consistency, not perfection. A missed day is fine; two in a row, restart.
Core · finding good topics early, with less effort

Ideas for CAS, TOK and the Extended Essay

Methods, not rules. His school's CAS coordinator, TOK teacher and EE supervisor have the final say.

CAS (Creativity, Activity, Service)

Finding ideas

  • Interest audit (15 min): list 10 things you already do or want to try, then sort them into Creativity, Activity and Service. Something you already do can count if you set a new challenge in it.
  • Skill × need: pair a skill (maths, physics, coding, design) with a real local need. Examples: maths tutoring for younger students, a website for a local association, a science demo afternoon at a primary school.
  • Outcome gaps: check the seven learning outcomes and choose experiences for the ones students usually lack: starting and planning something, and sticking with it over time.

Saving time

  • One experience can cover two strands (e.g. designing posters for a charity run = Creativity + Service).
  • Log evidence within 24 hours: one photo and a three-line reflection (what I did, what was hard, what I learned).
  • Fixed 10-minute CAS slot every Sunday to keep the portfolio current.
TOK (Theory of Knowledge)

Finding ideas

  • "How do we know?" journal: once a week, note a moment when knowledge was uncertain or disputed: a lab result, two history sources that disagree, a news story with competing claims.
  • Physics is a gift for TOK: measurement uncertainty, models that are "wrong but useful", and theories replaced over time are all strong TOK material.
  • Object walk (exhibition): photograph 20 specific things in one day at home, school and in the city, then match them to the 35 prompts. Specific beats generic: your lab data sheet from 14 October, not "a ruler".

Saving time

  • Choose the prompt first, then the objects. Keep a prompt only if all three objects give different angles on it.
  • Reuse material from subjects and the EE as TOK examples.
EE (Extended Essay)

Finding ideas

  • Curiosity week: for 7 days, write down every "I wonder why/how…" question from class, news or daily life. Pick the three that keep coming back.
  • Funnel: interest → subject → topic → a question narrow enough to answer in 4,000 words. "Energy" is too wide; "How does blade angle affect the power output of a small wind turbine?" is workable.
  • Interdisciplinary pathway: the 2027 guide allows two subjects together, e.g. Physics + Business (the cost-efficiency of home solar panels).

Feasibility test before committing

  • Can I get the data or sources (school lab, public datasets, library)?
  • Can it be done in about 40 hours of work?
  • Is there room to evaluate and compare interpretations? Discussion and evaluation is the highest-weighted criterion (8 of 30 marks).

Saving time

  • Read 2–3 good past essays in the subject for structure, not for ideas.
  • Keep one "researcher's reflection space" document from day one (sources, notes, dates, decisions). It feeds the 500-word reflection later.
  • Bring two candidate questions to the first supervisor meeting.
